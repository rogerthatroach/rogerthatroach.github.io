## Abstract

This article examines **closed-loop thinking** (observe &rarr; estimate &rarr; choose &rarr; act) across four distinct application domains: industrial combustion tuning, cloud document processing, financial process automation, and enterprise agentic AI. It presents the PSO mechanics used with 84 independent regression models and then uses the four-part loop as a bounded design heuristic for the later systems. The comparison is an analogy, not a mathematical isomorphism or a claim that guarantees transfer between domains.

## 1. Introduction

Applied AI practitioners often describe their careers as a sequence of projects, each with its own technologies, clients, and challenges. That framing can obscure recurring design questions: what is observed, what is estimated, what choice is made, who or what acts, and how outcomes are reviewed. Reusing those questions can accelerate design, but it does not erase domain-specific objectives, evidence standards, or failure modes.

This article starts with one actual **closed-loop optimization** system, then compares its design questions with three later projects spanning more than eight years of applied work:

## 2. Closed-Loop Optimization at Level 1

### 2.1 Working Definition

<TheoremBlock variant="definition" number={1} title="Closed-Loop Optimization System">

  </div>
  <MathBlock html={tex("\\mathcal{S} = (\\mathcal{Z},\\; \\mathcal{M},\\; \\mathcal{O},\\; \\mathcal{A})", true)} />
  <div className="mb-2">where:</div>
  <ul className="list-inside list-disc space-y-1">
    <li>
      <InlineEquation html={tex("\\mathcal{Z}: \\text{Environment} \\rightarrow \\mathcal{X}")} /> is
      the <strong>sensing function</strong>, mapping environmental state to observed and engineered features
      <InlineEquation html={tex("\\mathcal{X} \\subseteq \\mathbb{R}^n")} />
    </li>
    <li>
      <InlineEquation html={tex("\\mathcal{M}: (\\mathcal{Z}_{\\text{state}}, \\mathcal{U}) \\rightarrow \\hat{\\mathcal{Y}}")} /> is
      the <strong>modeling function</strong>, mapping observed state and candidate controllable settings to predicted outputs
      <InlineEquation html={tex("\\hat{\\mathcal{Y}} \\subseteq \\mathbb{R}^m")} />
    </li>
    <li>
      <InlineEquation html={tex("\\mathcal{O}: \\mathcal{Z}_{\\text{state}} \\times \\mathcal{C} \\rightarrow \\mathcal{U}^*")} />

    </li>

/>

### 2.2 Operating Conditions

**Feedback is data, not automatic improvement.** Actuation changes the environment and creates new observations. When outcomes are captured, reviewed, and deliberately incorporated, those observations can support monitoring or model updates.

**Optimization is constrained.** Candidate controllable settings are searched only inside a feasible set defined by operational and safety constraints:

<MathBlock html={tex("\\mathcal{U}^* = \\underset{u \\in \\mathcal{F}}{\\text{argmin}}\\; J(u \\mid z) \\quad \\text{where} \\quad \\mathcal{F} = \\{u \\in \\mathcal{U} \\mid c_j(u,z) \\leq 0,\\; j = 1, \\ldots, p\\}", true)} />

**Actuation can remain human-gated.** In a high-stakes physical system, the optimizer proposes settings and an operator decides whether to apply or hold them. The gate brings context that the model and recorded features do not capture.

## 3. Level 1: Particle Swarm Optimization for Combustion Tuning

### 3.1 Physical System

Kansai Electric owns and operates the 1,800MW Maizuru coal-fired power station in Japan. This work focused on one of its two 900MW generating units. Pulverized coal is combusted in a boiler to generate steam, producing nitrogen oxides (NOx), sulfur oxides (SOx), and carbon monoxide (CO). Their concentrations depend on boiler input settings: fuel mix ratios, air flow rates, damper positions, and secondary air distributions. MHPS participated as an engineering and equipment partner, not as the station operator.

### 3.2 Sensing: The Input Space

The plant instruments 90+ sensors measuring:

- Temperature at multiple boiler zones (<InlineEquation html={tex("T_1, T_2, \\ldots, T_k")} />)
- Pressure readings (<InlineEquation html={tex("P_1, P_2, \\ldots")} />)
- Flow rates (fuel, air, steam)
- Emissions concentrations at stack outlets
- Operational parameters (load, efficiency)

After feature engineering (domain-specific transformations, temporal aggregations, and interaction terms developed with the mechanical engineer on the team), the model input separates observed or exogenous state <InlineEquation html={tex("z")} /> from controllable boiler settings <InlineEquation html={tex("u")} />:

<MathBlock html={tex("\\mathbf{x} = (\\mathbf{z}, \\mathbf{u}) \\in \\mathbb{R}^n", true)} />

Only <InlineEquation html={tex("u")} /> is searched by the optimizer. Sensor readings and exogenous conditions in <InlineEquation html={tex("z")} /> condition the prediction but are not treated as actuator settings.

### 3.3 Modeling: 84 Simultaneous Regression Models

Each output variable of interest (emissions at different measurement points, temperature readings, efficiency indicators) receives its own regression model. Let <InlineEquation html={tex("y_i")} /> denote the <InlineEquation html={tex("i")} />-th output variable:

<MathBlock html={tex("\\hat{y}_i = f_i(\\mathbf{z}, \\mathbf{u};\\; \\boldsymbol{\\beta}_i), \\quad i = 1, 2, \\ldots, 84", true)} />

The modeling task is to learn 84 parameter vectors <InlineEquation html={tex("\\{\\boldsymbol{\\beta}_1, \\boldsymbol{\\beta}_2, \\ldots, \\boldsymbol{\\beta}_{84}\\}")} /> from historical sensor data.

#### 3.3.1 Feature Selection

With <InlineEquation html={tex("n \\approx 90+")} /> candidate features and potential interaction terms, dimensionality reduction is necessary. We used correlation-based feature selection:

**Step 1: Feature-target correlation.** For each model <InlineEquation html={tex("i")} />, compute Pearson correlation between each feature <InlineEquation html={tex("x_j")} /> and target <InlineEquation html={tex("y_i")} />:

<MathBlock
  html={tex("r_{j,i} = \\frac{\\sum_{t=1}^{T}(x_j^{(t)} - \\bar{x}_j)(y_i^{(t)} - \\bar{y}_i)}{\\sqrt{\\sum_{t=1}^{T}(x_j^{(t)} - \\bar{x}_j)^2 \\cdot \\sum_{t=1}^{T}(y_i^{(t)} - \\bar{y}_i)^2}}", true)}
  label="(1)"
/>

Retain features where <InlineEquation html={tex("|r_{j,i}| > r_{\\min}")} /> for a configurable threshold <InlineEquation html={tex("r_{\\min}")} />.

**Step 2: Feature-feature correlation.** Among retained features, identify pairs with <InlineEquation html={tex("|r_{j,k}| > r_{\\text{collinear}}")} />. For collinear pairs, retain the feature with higher target correlation.

This yields a model-specific feature set <InlineEquation html={tex("\\mathbf{x}_i \\subseteq \\mathbf{x}")} /> for each of the 84 models; not all models use the same features.

#### 3.3.2 Model Selection: Multi-Indicator Evaluation

For each model <InlineEquation html={tex("f_i")} />, we evaluated multiple candidate algorithms (linear regression, polynomial regression, ridge, lasso, random forest, gradient boosting, SVR) using k-fold cross-validation with <InlineEquation html={tex("K = 5")} /> folds. The selection criteria were:

Coefficient of Determination (<InlineEquation html={tex("R^2")} />):

<MathBlock
  html={tex("R^2_i = 1 - \\frac{\\sum_{t=1}^{T}(y_i^{(t)} - \\hat{y}_i^{(t)})^2}{\\sum_{t=1}^{T}(y_i^{(t)} - \\bar{y}_i)^2}", true)}
  label="(2)"
/>

Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE):

<MathBlock
  html={tex("\\text{RMSE}_i = \\sqrt{\\frac{1}{T}\\sum_{t=1}^{T}(y_i^{(t)} - \\hat{y}_i^{(t)})^2}", true)}
  label="(3)"
/>

Mean Absolute Percentage Error (MAPE):

<MathBlock
  html={tex("\\text{MAPE}_i = \\frac{100}{T}\\sum_{t=1}^{T}\\left|\\frac{y_i^{(t)} - \\hat{y}_i^{(t)}}{y_i^{(t)}}\\right|", true)}
  label="(4)"
/>

Mean Absolute Error (MAE):

<MathBlock
  html={tex("\\text{MAE}_i = \\frac{1}{T}\\sum_{t=1}^{T}|y_i^{(t)} - \\hat{y}_i^{(t)}|", true)}
  label="(5)"
/>

Inter-Fold Variance (<InlineEquation html={tex("\\sigma^2_{\\text{fold}}")} />):

<MathBlock
  html={tex("\\sigma^2_{\\text{fold},i} = \\text{Var}(\\{R^2_{i,1},\\; R^2_{i,2},\\; \\ldots,\\; R^2_{i,K}\\})", true)}
  label="(6)"
/>

where <InlineEquation html={tex("R^2_{i,k}")} /> is the <InlineEquation html={tex("R^2")} /> score of model <InlineEquation html={tex("i")} /> on fold <InlineEquation html={tex("k")} />.

<TheoremBlock variant="definition" number={2} title="Model Selection Criterion">
  <div>
    The selected model for output <InlineEquation html={tex("i")} /> is:
  </div>
  <MathBlock html={tex("f_i^* = \\underset{f \\in \\mathcal{F}}{\\text{argmax}}\\; R^2_i(f) \\quad \\text{subject to} \\quad \\sigma^2_{\\text{fold},i}(f) < \\sigma_{\\max}", true)} />
</TheoremBlock>

The stability constraint (<InlineEquation html={tex("\\sigma^2_{\\text{fold}} < \\sigma_{\\max}")} />) keeps fold-to-fold variability visible during model selection. High inter-fold variance indicates unstable out-of-sample behavior; it does not by itself diagnose memorization. For a system where predictions inform physical-control recommendations, a more stable model with slightly lower peak performance can be preferable to one whose validation performance varies sharply by fold.

### 3.4 Optimization: Particle Swarm Optimization

#### 3.4.1 PSO Fundamentals

Particle Swarm Optimization (PSO), introduced by Kennedy and Eberhart <CitationRef id={1} />, is a population-based metaheuristic inspired by collective behavior in biological swarms. A population of <InlineEquation html={tex("N")} /> particles navigates the controllable-setting space, each evaluating a candidate solution at its position while the observed state <InlineEquation html={tex("z")} /> is held fixed for that recommendation cycle.

<TheoremBlock variant="definition" number={3} title="Particle State">
  <div className="mb-2">
    Each particle <InlineEquation html={tex("i")} /> at iteration <InlineEquation html={tex("t")} /> has:
  </div>
  <ul className="list-inside list-disc space-y-1">
    <li>
      Position: <InlineEquation html={tex("\\mathbf{u}_i^{(t)} \\in \\mathcal{U}")} /> (a candidate
      set of boiler input settings)
    </li>
    <li>
      Velocity: <InlineEquation html={tex("\\mathbf{v}_i^{(t)} \\in \\mathbb{R}^n")} /> (the
      direction and magnitude of movement)
    </li>
    <li>
      Personal best:
      <InlineEquation html={tex("\\mathbf{p}_i^{(t)} = \\underset{s \\leq t}{\\text{argmin}}\\; J(\\mathbf{u}_i^{(s)} \\mid z)")} />
    </li>
    <li>
      Global best:
      <InlineEquation html={tex("\\mathbf{g}^{(t)} = \\underset{i,\\, s \\leq t}{\\text{argmin}}\\; J(\\mathbf{u}_i^{(s)} \\mid z)")} />
    </li>
  </ul>
</TheoremBlock>

#### 3.4.2 Update Equations

The velocity and position update rules are:

<MathBlock
  html={tex("\\mathbf{v}_i^{(t+1)} = w \\cdot \\mathbf{v}_i^{(t)} + c_1 r_1 \\cdot (\\mathbf{p}_i^{(t)} - \\mathbf{u}_i^{(t)}) + c_2 r_2 \\cdot (\\mathbf{g}^{(t)} - \\mathbf{u}_i^{(t)})", true)}
  label="(7)"
/>

<MathBlock
  html={tex("\\mathbf{u}_i^{(t+1)} = \\mathbf{u}_i^{(t)} + \\mathbf{v}_i^{(t+1)}", true)}
  label="(8)"
/>

where:

- <InlineEquation html={tex("w")} /> is the **inertia weight** (controls exploration vs. exploitation)
- <InlineEquation html={tex("c_1")} /> is the **cognitive coefficient** (attraction to personal best)
- <InlineEquation html={tex("c_2")} /> is the **social coefficient** (attraction to global best)
- <InlineEquation html={tex("r_1, r_2 \\sim \\text{Uniform}(0, 1)")} /> are stochastic scaling factors

The inertia and acceleration parameters control the empirical balance between broad search and local refinement <CitationRef id={2} />. A decreasing inertia schedule can shift that balance over the run, but these settings do not establish a global-optimum guarantee; convergence behavior depends on the objective, constraints, initialization, and stopping rule.

#### 3.4.3 Multi-Objective Formulation

The combustion tuning objective is to minimize pollutant emissions simultaneously:

<MathBlock
  html={tex("\\text{minimize}_{\\mathbf{u}} \\quad J(\\mathbf{u} \\mid \\mathbf{z}) = \\alpha_1 \\cdot \\hat{y}_{\\text{NOx}}(\\mathbf{z},\\mathbf{u}) + \\alpha_2 \\cdot \\hat{y}_{\\text{SOx}}(\\mathbf{z},\\mathbf{u}) + \\alpha_3 \\cdot \\hat{y}_{\\text{CO}}(\\mathbf{z},\\mathbf{u})", true)}
  label="(9)"
/>

subject to:

<MathBlock
  html={tex("\\begin{aligned} u_j^{\\min} &\\leq u_j \\leq u_j^{\\max} & \\forall j & & &\\text{(validated setting bounds)} \\\\ y_{\\text{load}}(\\mathbf{z},\\mathbf{u}) &\\geq y_{\\text{load}}^{\\min} & & & &\\text{(minimum load constraint)} \\\\ T_{\\text{boiler}}(\\mathbf{z},\\mathbf{u}) &\\leq T_{\\max} & & & &\\text{(safety constraint)} \\end{aligned}", true)}
  label="(10)"
/>

where <InlineEquation html={tex("\\alpha_1, \\alpha_2, \\alpha_3")} /> are weighting coefficients reflecting the relative importance of each pollutant (configurable based on regulatory priorities), and <InlineEquation html={tex("\\hat{y}_{\\text{NOx}}, \\hat{y}_{\\text{SOx}}, \\hat{y}_{\\text{CO}}")} /> are the trained regression models for each pollutant.

Why PSO over gradient-based methods?

PSO was a practical fit for a nonlinear objective without a convenient analytic gradient and for exploring multiple candidate regions in parallel <CitationRef id={3} />. It remains a stochastic heuristic, not a proof of global optimality. Multiple seeds, constraint checks, sensitivity review, and operator judgment are part of using its recommendations safely.

### 3.5 Actuation: Human-in-the-Loop

The PSO output is a set of model-recommended candidate boiler settings: specific fuel ratios, air-flow rates, and damper positions. Plant operators reviewed the recommendations and decided whether to make the physical adjustments.

The human-in-the-loop design was deliberate. On a 900MW generating unit, automated control changes carry catastrophic risk: incorrect settings could damage equipment worth hundreds of millions of dollars or cause safety incidents. The operators provided a validation layer the model could not: awareness of transient plant states, equipment wear, and operational context not captured in sensor data.

### 3.6 Results

The system achieved:

- **$3M annual cost savings** attributed to the optimization program
- **Operator-reviewed recommendations** over controllable boiler settings, conditioned on observed plant state
- **A reusable operating pattern:** combine predictive models, bounded search, constraint checks, and operator judgment

### 4.5 The Abstraction Gradient

**Observation.** As the abstraction level increases:

1. **The sensing function becomes more semantic.** Physical sensors (<InlineEquation html={tex("\\mathbb{R}^n")} />) &rarr; document parsers &rarr; data pipelines &rarr; embedding models (<InlineEquation html={tex("\\mathbb{R}^d")} />). The inputs evolve from physical measurements to semantic representations.
2. **The modeling function becomes more compositional.** Single regression models &rarr; extraction pipelines &rarr; transformation chains &rarr; model-assisted graph orchestration. Each level composes more processing stages.
3. **The choice function changes form.** Bounded numerical search &rarr; quality objectives &rarr; process targets &rarr; retrieval, tool, clarification, and abstention policies. Later stages should not be mistaken for numerical optimizers.
4. **The actuation function becomes more autonomous.** Human-operated controls &rarr; semi-automated pipelines &rarr; automated reports &rarr; AI-guided decisions with human oversight. But full autonomy is never the goal. Human judgment remains in the loop.

## 5. Pattern Recognition as Design Methodology

### 5.1 Practical Benefits

Recognizing the closed-loop pattern in a new domain provides immediate architectural scaffolding:

**Accelerated design.** When approaching a new enterprise AI problem, asking “What is observed? What estimates are produced? What choice follows? Who or what acts?” structures the solution space and reveals design choices.

**Risk discovery.** The questions transfer; the guarantees do not. Asking where human review belongs, what the reviewer can actually observe, and how disagreement is recorded can reveal missing controls. Whether those controls improve outcomes must be tested in each domain.

**Communication.** The four-question frame can make a complex AI workflow easier to discuss with non-technical stakeholders, provided the analogy is not presented as evidence that the systems share control properties.

### 5.2 Limitations

This is a comparison tool, not a control theorem. Mapping a workflow to observe &rarr; estimate &rarr; choose &rarr; act does not tell you which embedding model to use, how to design orchestration, how to enforce entitlements, or whether feedback improves the system. Those questions require domain-specific evidence and controls.

## 6. Related Work

**Particle Swarm Optimization.** Kennedy and Eberhart <CitationRef id={1} /> introduced PSO; Shi and Eberhart <CitationRef id={2} /> added inertia weight for improved convergence. Clerc and Kennedy <CitationRef id={4} /> provided convergence analysis. PSO remains widely used in engineering optimization due to its simplicity and effectiveness on non-convex landscapes.

**Digital Twins.** Grieves <CitationRef id={5} /> introduced the Digital Twin concept; Tao et al. <CitationRef id={6} /> formalized it for manufacturing. Our combustion tuning system predates the widespread adoption of the term but implements the core idea: a data-driven model of a physical system used for optimization and control.

**Multi-Objective Optimization.** Coello Coello et al. <CitationRef id={7} /> survey evolutionary approaches to multi-objective optimization, including Pareto-based methods. Our weighted-sum approach is a simplification; Pareto-optimal methods (NSGA-II <CitationRef id={8} />) would allow exploring the full tradeoff frontier between pollutant types.

**Agentic AI Architectures.** LangGraph <CitationRef id={9} />, AutoGen <CitationRef id={10} />, and CrewAI provide orchestration frameworks. Their observe-route-evaluate cycles are useful comparison points, but they are not equivalent to classical feedback control and do not inherit its guarantees.

**Architectural Patterns in Software.** Gamma et al. <CitationRef id={11} /> established design patterns as a communication and design tool. This article applies that communicative idea at the system level while keeping the cross-domain comparison explicitly heuristic.

## 7. Conclusion

Closed-loop thinking is a bounded design heuristic: observe, estimate, choose, act. It can help decompose systems as different as industrial combustion control and enterprise AI, but the resemblance is analogical. Each system still needs its own objective, evidence, controls, and failure analysis.

The most useful question is what happens after a model produces an estimate. In the plant system, operators reviewed model-recommended candidate settings. In enterprise workflows, people may review a structured draft, a calculation, or a comparison. Making that action boundary explicit clarifies both the intended value and the controls the system still needs.
