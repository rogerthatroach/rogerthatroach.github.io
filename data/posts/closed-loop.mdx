## 1. The question after a prediction

A model output is not yet a useful system. Someone still has to decide what the estimate means, whether it is safe to act on, and what happens after that action changes the world.

That boundary was tangible in my first production machine-learning work. Regression models estimated how a large generating unit might respond to candidate combustion settings. Particle Swarm Optimization searched for promising settings. Plant operators decided whether to apply them. The models did not operate the plant, and the optimizer did not possess the operating context held by the people in the control room.

That experience left me with four questions that have remained useful across later systems:

1. **Observe:** What can the system actually see?
2. **Estimate:** What is inferred rather than directly measured?
3. **Choose:** Which policy, objective, or person selects the next step?
4. **Act:** Who changes the physical or organizational state?

These questions are a design heuristic, not a claim that every data pipeline or AI workflow is a feedback-control system. The distinction matters because analogies become dangerous when they carry guarantees from one domain into another.

## 2. What makes a loop real

A real feedback loop has more than four boxes arranged in a circle. An action changes the environment; the changed environment is observed again; the observed outcome can be compared with the expected one; and that evidence reaches the people or processes responsible for the next decision.

Three qualifications keep the term honest:

- **Feedback is captured evidence, not automatic learning.** A system does not improve merely because new data exists. Outcomes have to be associated with the action that preceded them, reviewed, and deliberately incorporated into monitoring, rules, or model updates.
- **Choice is constrained.** An optimizer searches within a permitted operating region. A workflow router chooses among registered paths. A reviewer can decline both. Those are different choice mechanisms with different evidence requirements.
- **Actuation may remain human-gated.** In a high-stakes setting, a recommendation can be the correct system output. The accountable operator decides whether the surrounding conditions support acting on it.

## 3. The plant system

### 3.1 Context and responsibility

Kansai Electric owns and operates the 1,800MW Maizuru coal-fired power station in Japan. The work focused on one 900MW generating unit. MHPS participated as an engineering and equipment partner; it was not the plant operator.

Combustion performance depends on a mixture of measured conditions and controllable settings. Sensor readings describe temperatures, pressures, flows, load, and emissions. Candidate settings include variables such as fuel mix, airflow, and damper positions. Keeping those categories separate is fundamental: an optimizer may search a controllable setting, but it cannot choose the weather, equipment condition, or every transient state represented by the sensors.

### 3.2 Eighty-four regression models

The system used 84 regression models to estimate relevant plant outputs under the observed state and a candidate set of controllable settings. Different outputs can depend on different subsets of the available measurements, so feature selection and validation were performed per model rather than assuming one universal input set.

Model evaluation considered both predictive error and stability across held-out folds. The purpose was not to crown one algorithm with a single headline score. A model that performs well on average but varies sharply across folds can be a poor basis for recommendations. The engineering question is whether its behavior is stable enough, over the tested operating region, to contribute to a bounded search whose result will still be reviewed by an operator.

The evaluation surface is evidence about tested data, not a statement that the model understands the plant. Changes in fuel, maintenance state, sensor quality, or operating regime can move the system outside that evidence. Monitoring and operator judgment remain necessary after model selection.

### 3.3 Searching for candidate settings

Particle Swarm Optimization (PSO) is a population-based search method introduced by Kennedy and Eberhart <CitationRef id={1} />. Each particle represents one candidate combination of controllable settings. The search evaluates those candidates through the regression models, remembers promising positions, and moves the population toward better regions while retaining some exploration. Inertia and acceleration parameters affect that balance <CitationRef id={2} />.

For the plant system, PSO was useful because the combined objective was nonlinear and did not offer a convenient analytic gradient. It could explore multiple candidate regions while respecting configured setting bounds and operating constraints. It remained a stochastic heuristic, not a proof of a global optimum. Initialization, parameter choices, stopping rules, model error, and the shape of the feasible region all affect the result <CitationRef id={3} />.

The output was a set of candidate combustion settings, not a command. Constraint checks removed candidates outside configured limits, and plant operators reviewed the remaining recommendation against the current unit state. They could apply it, hold it, or choose a different response.

That operating boundary was not a compromise around an otherwise autonomous design. It was the design. The program was associated with approximately $3 million in annual savings while keeping the operator as the final action gate.

## 4. Where feedback enters

If an operator applies a recommendation, the plant moves into a new state. Sensors then record the resulting temperatures, flows, load, emissions, and other conditions. Those observations can be compared with the estimates that informed the recommendation.

But several links have to remain intact for that comparison to be useful:

- the recommendation and its model/configuration versions;
- the settings the operator actually applied, which may differ from the proposal;
- the time window in which the response is evaluated;
- relevant operating conditions and interventions during that window; and
- the review decision about whether the observed difference warrants monitoring, a rule change, or retraining.

Without those associations, new sensor data is merely later data. It cannot explain which action produced which outcome. And even with them, a single before-and-after observation does not isolate causality in a complex industrial process.

This is why the human gate and the evidence loop belong in the same architecture. The operator contributes context before action. The recorded outcome contributes evidence after action. Neither is replaced by the regression models or the optimizer.

## 5. Testing the heuristic in later systems

The four questions still help outside industrial control, but the answers reveal why the later systems are not all closed loops.

The comparison prevents a common category error. Commodity Tax has an explicit correction and rerun path, but its core calculation is not a model estimate. PAR Assist can return missing coverage to clarification, but the human-reviewed draft does not actuate a physical environment. Aegis can abstain on ambiguous KPI candidates, but an abstention policy is not numerical optimization. Astraeus can constrain entitlements and calculation in code, but its answer phrasing remains model-mediated.

## 6. Where the analogy breaks

The heuristic becomes misleading when it turns every processing stage into a control concept.

**Observation is not always a sensor reading.** A document chunk, database row, or user request is an application input, but the way it was selected and represented already shapes what the system can see.

**Not every transformation is an estimate.** Commodity Tax applies configured rules and arithmetic. Calling that step a model blurs the difference between deterministic computation and uncertain inference.

**Not every choice is optimization.** A reviewed SQL template, entitlement policy, clarification threshold, or graph edge can determine the next step without solving an objective function.

**Not every output is actuation.** A structured draft or analysis may inform a decision while leaving the accountable human entirely outside the software's control boundary.

**Not every correction closes a learning loop.** Feedback has to be captured, attributed, reviewed, and used. Otherwise it remains an interaction outcome rather than evidence that changes future system behavior.

Those distinctions are not pedantry. They determine which tests are meaningful, which failures can be detected, and who remains responsible when the system is uncertain.

## 7. A practical design review

When evaluating a new applied-ML or AI workflow, I now use the four questions with a second layer of challenge:

1. **Observe:** What is directly recorded, what is selected or transformed, and what relevant context remains invisible?
2. **Estimate:** Which outputs are probabilistic, which are deterministic, and what evidence supports each boundary?
3. **Choose:** Is the next step selected by an optimizer, a rule, a model, or a person? What alternatives can it reject or abstain from?
4. **Act:** Who is accountable for changing the physical or business state, and can they inspect the evidence they need?
5. **Return:** Is the outcome associated with the action strongly enough to support monitoring or learning, or does the loop end at delivery?

The fifth question is the safeguard. It stops a visually circular diagram from being mistaken for an operating feedback system.

## 8. Conclusion

The most important design question often begins after the model predicts. A useful system has to connect an estimate or transformation to a bounded decision, an accountable actor, and evidence about what happened next.

At Maizuru, 84 regression models and PSO supported operator-reviewed combustion-setting candidates. The operators remained responsible for plant action, and later sensor readings could inform review only when tied back to what was proposed and applied. In later enterprise systems, the same questions help locate clarification, correction, and human review without pretending that every workflow is a closed loop.

The questions transfer. The mechanisms, evidence, and guarantees do not.
