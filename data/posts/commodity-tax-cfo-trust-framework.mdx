Commodity Tax was a process-automation project, not an AI system. Its job was to turn a months-long financial workflow into a repeatable calculation while keeping analysts able to question the result.

Two decisions shaped that operating model. The first concerned calculation: where should a multi-stage transformation run, and how should it remain maintainable? The second concerned review: what should an analyst see when a returned value looks wrong?

## Four operating constraints

- The calculation needed to process the full in-scope General Ledger population through extraction, reconciliation, category mapping, aggregation, and return preparation.
- Material mappings and corrections needed an explicit review path.
- Finance users already worked in Tableau and spreadsheets; introducing an unfamiliar application would add adoption work to an already difficult process change.
- A final number without an investigation path would leave analysts dependent on the delivery team whenever something looked wrong.

These constraints did not dictate one implementation. They did make repeatability, maintainability, and inspectability more important than novelty.

## Decision 1: use PySpark for the calculation path

A PySpark pipeline on the approved enterprise data platform encoded the five conceptual stages as a repeatable transformation rather than leaving them distributed across manual workbooks and handoffs.

A lighter Python implementation with explicit batching would have been easier to iterate on locally, but it would also have introduced batch-boundary and memory-management work into a process that already had difficult reconciliation logic. Keeping the calculation on the approved platform also made the operating and support boundary clearer for the broader delivery team.

PySpark fit the combination of full-cycle processing, repeatable transformations, and team maintainability in this environment; it was not a universal preference.

Spark development has a slower feedback loop than local dataframe work, and distributed execution does not make business rules correct. Source completeness, joins, mappings, and aggregation logic still required testing and finance review.

## Decision 2: put inspection beside calculation

Configured Tableau views sat alongside relevant pipeline stages. The return remained the deliverable; the views gave analysts a familiar place to inspect recorded intermediate state and follow supported associations toward mappings and source records.

A final report or output-only dashboard would have been simpler to deliver, but it would have answered only “what number did the process produce?” A custom audit application offered more control, yet would have created another product to build, adopt, secure, and maintain.

When an analyst questioned a value, the system needed to narrow the disagreement without pretending to resolve it automatically. A familiar inspection surface made that possible with less adoption cost than a new interface.

The Tableau views required their own join, filter, access, and semantic checks. They could expose only the relationships retained by the calculation path. A visible trace did not prove that the source population was complete, a mapping was approved, or a returned amount was correct.

## The operating loop

The operating unit was a review loop: question a value, inspect the recorded evidence, reconcile or correct the issue, rerun the affected calculation, and review the result again.

That loop preserved the responsibility boundary. Finance partners owned the meaning of categories and exceptions. The delivery team implemented and tested the transformations. Analysts investigated anomalies and reviewed corrections. The software made the path more repeatable and the evidence easier to inspect; it did not replace accountable judgment.

## Outcome and residual risk

By Q4 2023, the process completed in roughly 90 minutes rather than months and supported an allocation of roughly $600M. The work received the CFO Group RBC Quarterly Team Award.

Those outcomes came from the whole delivery: codified mappings, PySpark processing, configured inspection, finance partnership, testing, and operating review.

The main residual risk was evidence quality. A dropped identifier, changed mapping without the matching version, inconsistent join, or omitted source record could interrupt an investigation path. The honest system response in those cases was to surface an evidence gap and return the issue to reconciliation, not construct a confident explanation.

## Decision rule

1. Choose the calculation technology for the actual processing and support constraints, not for architectural fashion.
2. Keep review surfaces separate from the calculation while building them from the same retained records.
3. Treat inspectability as support for investigation, never as proof of correctness.
4. Make correction, rerun, and accountable human review part of the operating design.

The tools will change. The distinction between computing a value, exposing the available evidence, and deciding what that evidence means should remain explicit.
