The Commodity Tax automation in RBC&rsquo;s CFO Group reduced a months-long process to roughly 90 minutes. The work supported an allocation of roughly $600M and received a CFO Group RBC Quarterly Team Award in Q4 2023.

The delivery combined calculation with the practical work of auditing and debugging a complex financial workflow. Every run saved its inputs and intermediate outputs. Multiple Excel reports and dynamic Tableau dashboards at each stage let the team inspect what the process had produced before looking only at the final return.

## The workflow

The Commodity Tax process involved General Ledger extraction, reconciliation, category mapping, aggregation, and return preparation. These five stages summarize a much larger Dataiku scenario containing Python and Spark code.

I led development and stakeholder engagement, working with finance partners who supplied the meaning of categories and exceptions. Automating the transformations did not remove that collaboration: a calculation could execute successfully while applying a rule incorrectly or receiving incomplete source data.

The reporting and saved-data paths made those questions easier to investigate during delivery and operation.

## Preserve the work of each run

The scenario saved every input and resulting intermediate file. Shared-storage folders were organized by run date and time, with corresponding partitions on the enterprise data platform. PySpark made the partitioned data easier to retrieve and navigate, alongside the saved files.

That organization gave an investigation a concrete starting point: identify the run, open the relevant stage data, and compare its input and output. An earlier run remained available to inspect after later processing had taken place.

## Reports and dashboards at every stage

Excel remained part of the delivered system. Each stage produced multiple reports alongside dynamic Tableau dashboards for audit and debugging. The reports exposed stage results for examination; the dashboards provided interactive ways to explore the data.

The underlying inputs and intermediates were also saved, so investigation could extend beyond a report or dashboard. If an amount looked wrong, finance and engineering could examine the relevant run, compare stage outputs, and inspect the transformation or mapping involved.

The comparison needed to stay within the same run. A report from one execution and a dashboard or dataset from another could create an apparent discrepancy. Maintaining the stage outputs and checking the selected run, filters, and aggregations remained part of operating the workflow.

Reports, dashboards, and saved datasets served different parts of the same review. A disagreement still required interpretation: whether it came from source data, a business rule, its implementation, or the presentation of a result.

## The outcome and its limits

By Q4 2023, the process completed in roughly 90 minutes rather than months. The result came from the whole delivery: Python and Spark transformations, run-organized data retention, stage reports, dashboards, testing, and finance collaboration.

Saving the inputs and intermediates made the calculation inspectable. It did not prove that every input was complete or every rule was correct. Analysts and the delivery team still had to investigate discrepancies, review corrections, and check the resulting output.

## What I would change

I would expose the mapping-rule surface earlier. Reports and dashboards supported inspection, but mapping changes still moved through code and redeployment. A reviewed interface for proposing changes could have made that collaboration more direct while preserving approval and version control.

The useful lesson was to make the work of a run available for examination: retain its data, expose stage results in forms people can use, and keep a practical route from a questioned number back to the calculation.
